## Editorial Perspective

- This edition confesses Holy Scripture as God's inerrant word and the
  word-for-word inspired revelation given in the original writings.
- This project gives deliberate weight to Septuagintal and other early textual
  witnesses because complete Greek biblical codices such as Vaticanus and
  Sinaiticus are centuries earlier than complete medieval Masoretic codices
  such as Leningrad.
- This project also gives serious attention to Hebrew textual plurality before
  medieval standardization, especially in light of Dead Sea Scroll evidence.
- This edition argues that many New Testament quotations align more closely
  with Septuagintal or other non-Masoretic forms than with the later medieval
  Masoretic tradition.
- This edition also treats some later Jewish textual standardization and
  revision as theologically significant for Christian readers, especially where
  christological, angelological, or supernatural themes appear reduced or
  redirected in later forms of the text.
- This rationale includes passages such as Genesis 6 and other texts where
  older readings help illuminate biblical supernatural themes and later New
  Testament usage.
- This is an editorial rationale page, not an attempt to settle every point of
  modern text-critical debate inside one preface.

## Research Notes

- Earliest complete Greek biblical codices preserving broad Septuagintal text:
  4th-5th century AD.
- Oldest complete medieval Masoretic codex commonly used in printed editions:
  Leningrad Codex, AD 1008.
- Dead Sea Scrolls show earlier Hebrew textual diversity long before medieval
  Masoretic stabilization.
- Jude 14-15 explicitly cites Enochic prophecy.
- Many New Testament quotations are widely recognized as being closer to the
  Septuagint than to the later Masoretic wording.
